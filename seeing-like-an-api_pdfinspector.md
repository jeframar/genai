Abstract This study examines the challenges that researchers faced while accessing, interpreting, and assessing data accessed via Meta’s now-sunset CrowdTangle (CT) application programming interface (API). Drawing on interviews with 20 academic users, we introduce the framework of Seeing like an API to illustrate how CT’stechnicaldesign,gov- ernance rules, and platform-mediated knowledge networks shaped what researchers could see, ask, and conclude about platform activity. Participants adapted to limitations such as follower-count thresholds, the exclusion of com- ment data, and opaque content removal through three recurring strategies: optimizing the API’s capabilities, extend- ing data visibility with supplementary methods, and verifying outputs against external sources. These adaptations were developed in response to ongoing uncertainty about data completeness and provenance, which constrained both the scope of feasible research questions and the reliability of resulting analyses. Our findings show how platform- controlled data infrastructures shape research design, collaboration, and epistemic norms, and they inform emerging models for independent, third-party-regulated data access, such as those envisioned in the European Union’s Digital Services Act.

Introduction

Application programming interfaces (APIs) are widely used data infrastructures that enable researchers to access large datasets for analyzing public opinion, social movements, misinformation, and other phenomena online. APIs have become indispensable in social media research by structur- ing data exchanges between platforms and external actors. However, their utility remains shaped and constrained by proprietary access models that grant platforms sole author- ity over data visibility and conditions of use. Within the

U.S. context, platforms typically retain full control over data access, significantly limiting researchers’ ability to conduct independent, reproducible analyses (Hemphill et al., 2021; John and Nissenbaum, 2019). These platform- imposed limitations can introduce structural biases into scholarly work by influencing the scope of feasible research questions and constraining knowledge production (Acker and Donovan, 2019; Allen et al., 2021). Emerging data governance models, such as the European Union’s Digital Services Act (DSA), illustrate alternative
approaches where independent third parties like governmental bodies mediate access to platform data. Such arrangements could mitigate biases that are common in platform-controlled APIs, where platforms themselves decide what data to release and how it is presented. For instance, Facebook’sAdArchive (now Meta Ad Library) exemplifies challenges in platform- mediated transparency initiatives, offering only partial target- ing information, directing research toward visible data and away from opaque dimensions of platform operations (Bruns, 2019). Similarly, tiered access models, as previously exemplified by Twitter’s Academic Research Product Track, reinforce academic hierarchies by privileging well-resourced

## Literature review

Social media APIs provide structured and standardized ac- cess to the vast amounts of data generated by user interac- tions on platforms. Despite their utility, there has been considerable criticism regarding the risks of using social media data uncritically in academic research (boyd and Crawford, 2012; Kitchin, 2014). A central critique is the re- liance on platform-provided metrics as proxies for human behavior, as observable online interactions may not accur- ately reflect offline behaviors (Rogers, 2018) and are them- selves often influenced by algorithmic interventions (Seaver, 2019). Moreover, the transient nature of social media data also introduces challenges: platform changes, content moderation practices, and user behaviors such as deletion or archiving can disrupt the reliability of datasets (Walker, 2017; Zubiaga, 2018). These conditions stand in stark contrast to the standards of the scholarly archival com- munity, where contemporary theory and practice emphasize order, provenance, and retrievability, and highlight the rela- tive limitations of social media platforms’ curation stan- dards (Acker and Donovan, 2019; Gilliland, 2014). often unclear or unavailable for researchers using data ob- tained through APIs. In this context, social media APIs pro- vide visibility into data that may become unstable, undergo alterations, or disappear entirely due to factors that are often beyond researchers’ control. In addition to technical instability, APIs also enforce “re- gimes of access” that structure who can see what data, and under what conditions, raising concerns about the validity and representativeness of platform-provided data (Bruns and Burgess, 2016; Hargittai, 2020). Data can be intention- ally or unintentionally made unavailable. Intentionally, platforms may withhold data from certain parties, such as Facebook’s “reach” metric. Unintentionally, platforms may lack data on specific interactions, like user disconnec- tivity (e.g. unfollowing) (John and Nissenbaum, 2019), while prioritizing data beneficial to their commercial inter- ests, such as connectivity metrics (Baym, 2013; Gerlitz and Helmond, 2013; Wu and Taneja, 2021). This selective visi- bility extends to who can access what, creating tiers of data access across academic, commercial, and public stake- holders (John and Nissenbaum, 2019). As a result, research- ers may unintentionally work with data that is filtered in ways that introduce bias, leading to findings that cast plat- forms in an overly unfavorable light (Allen et al., 2021) or an overly favorable light (Acker and Donovan, 2019). The design and governance of social media APIs are shaped by the commercial interests and power dynamics embedded within platform infrastructures (van der Vlist et al., 2022). Although initially developed for commercial use, APIs have been widely adopted by scholars as tools for studying online behavior. However, their design, access, and continued availability remain tightly governed by plat- form policies that are often opaque, subject to change, and seldom tailored to the needs of academic inquiry (Wu and Taneja, 2021). Moreover, the legal frameworks (i.e. the terms and conditions) attached to social media data are com- plex and continually evolving in ways that limit how data can effectively be used, especially when compared to more stable data sources traditionally used in scholarly re- search (Hemphill et al., 2021). Over the last several years, scholars have contended that the reliability and openness of APIs for research purposes have noticeably declined (Rieder and Hofmann, 2020). Platforms routinely modify, replace, or consolidate their APIs, narrowing both the scope of accessible data and the functionalities available to researchers. While APIs remain in use, many now offer restricted access and diminished capabilities. This has spurred a growing body of work ad- dressing how researchers can operate in what has been de- scribed as a “post-API” (Freelon, 2018) or “API-calypse” (Bruns, 2019) environment. These conditions present un- ique epistemic challenges for researchers: when platforms alter API specifications or remove endpoints altogether, they not only disrupt data flows but also render previously

## Application programming interfaces as objects of

study

Beyond their functional roles as interfaces, social media APIs have increasingly been studied as meaningful techno- logical and sociopolitical constructs. Helmond (2015) de- scribes APIs as the “technological glue” of the social web, in that they not only enable interoperability across platforms but also support data-sharing business models central to the platform economy. Similarly, Bucher (2013) conceptualizes APIs as protological objects, foundational elements that establish the rules governing digital environ- ments. The term “protological” stems from “protocol,” drawing attention to the standards and conventions that structure communication and interaction in networked sys- tems. From this perspective, APIs do more than transmit data: they embed specific assumptions and constraints about how data should be accessed, shared, and controlled. As such, APIs are best understood as quasi-objects, or objects that mediate the relationships between human and non- human actors, influencing not just technology infrastructure but also economic logics like ownership and licensing, along with user practices and social norms and imaginaries (Bucher, 2013). Unlike static tools, APIs derive meaning from being embedded within broader sociotechnical sys- tems. Their functionality is defined through dynamic inter- actions across platforms, services, and user communities. As scholars have noted, APIs are not neutral or passive technical tools; specific values, priorities, and power dy- namics are embedded into their protocols (Snodgrass and Soon, 2019). Understanding this entanglement of technical and social dimensions is essential in the context of aca- demic research, as it affects not only what data become ac- cessible but also how knowledge is constructed, interpreted, and legitimized within these environments. While existing literature has offered important critiques of the uncritical use of APIs and the epistemic risks they pose, less attention has been given to how researchers navigate these challenges

in practice. Identifying the limitations of API-provided data is essential, but it is equally important to examine the strat- egies researchers use to adapt to and work around these con- straints. As Vis (2013) notes, while methodological challenges in social media research are often acknowledged in theory, they are not always addressed in applied settings. This study addresses this gap by focusing on the practical aspects of API use, specifically the techniques and methods researchers employ to optimize, extend, and validate the data obtained through these infrastructures. Instead of con- centrating solely on the data that APIs restrict or omit, we center our analysis on the research practices and methodo- logical adaptations such absences inspire, along with their consequences for epistemic integrity.

## Seeing like an application programming interface

In Seeing Like a State, Scott (1998) highlights how states and bureaucracies simplify and impose schematic visions on the natural and social worlds, often forsaking their com- plexity and interdependency in order to govern them more effectively. The concept of “seeing like” is grounded in standpoint epistemology, which posits that all forms of ob- servation are shaped by particular positions and perspec- tives (Haraway, 1988). Vis (2013) also employs this metaphor to illustrate how the data-sharing business model of social media platforms enables and incentivizes plat- forms to construct a “synoptic view” into certain digital worlds in ways that have consequences for the users who create the data and for the researchers working with them. In contrast, Vertesi’s (2012) Seeing like a Rover and Singh and Jackson’s (2021) Seeing Like an Infrastructure propose a more distributed and networked approach to un- derstanding visibility and schema imposition. Vertesi ex- amines how visibility and agency are actively constructed from the ground up by the Mars Rover engineering team, challenging the notion that visibility must be imposed from a top-down perspective. Instead, it is presented as an emergent property of collaborative and situational interac- tions. Similarly, Singh and Jackson (2021) explore how the Aadhaar biometric identification system in India encap- sulates a variety of perspectives from its diverse partici- pants, demonstrating how infrastructure itself can shape and is shaped by the distributed actions of its users. These perspectives challenge the notion of visibility as fixed or hierarchical, instead portraying it as emergent, contingent, and co-constructed within sociotechnical systems. Our framework of Seeing like an API both departs from and synthesizes these two traditions. Unlike Scott’s (1998) top-down model, in which a centralized authority renders a complex world legible, API-mediated seeing requires the continuous, distributed labor of its subjects to remain func- tional. Methods

To answer our research questions, we conducted semi- structured interviews with researchers (N = 20) who utilized the Meta-acquired CT API and analytics tool for research. We selected researchers at various stages in their careers in eight different countries and across several disciplines to increase the institutional and disciplinary diversity in our sample. We conducted interviews until saturation was reached (Corbin and Strauss, 2015). To gain additional in- sight and background information on CT, we also con- ducted interviews with former members of the CT team (n = 3) and early adopters or power users of the tool from outside academia (n = 4). These supplementary interviews were not included in the formal data analysis but informed background knowledge and interpretation of findings. To protect participant anonymity, additional demographic de- tails are intentionally omitted. Eligibility criteria included having at least one peer- reviewed publication that used CT for data collection. Researchers were contacted via email between July and September 2021. Interviews were conducted remotely by the first author on the Zoom platform from July to October 2021 due to the COVID-19 pandemic and geo- graphical constraints. ## Research site

CT began as an independent company before being ac- quired by Meta in 2016. It became a key tool for monitoring public content on Facebook and Instagram, widely used by journalists and researchers to track link sharing, engage- ment, and trends. CT’s infrastructure included three main components: a web-based search interface, a browser exten- sion, and an API. While participants sometimes used the web tool or extension, this study focuses on the API, which was the most consistently employed for large-scale, pro- grammatic data collection. CT’s design offered distinctive affordances compared to APIs from other platforms, particularly in its coverage of or- ganic (non-paid) content from public Facebook pages, groups, and Instagram profiles. However, it also imposed notable restrictions—such as follower-count thresholds and exclusion of comment data—that shaped research de- sign and feasibility. Combined with Meta’s exclusive con- trol over access, these constraints positioned CT as both a valuable resource for and a gatekeeper of social media research. Since its acquisition, CT has attracted considerable media and academic attention, particularly regarding its phase-out (Heath, 2021; Smalley, 2022). The timing of our study provides an important backdrop. All interviews were conducted between July and October 2021, prior to Meta’s March 2024 announcement that CT would be dis- continued in August 2024. Nonetheless, during the data col- lection period, participants were already experiencing shrinking access—for example, Meta had stopped respond- ing to new access applications. This moment of infrastruc- tural uncertainty offered a unique vantage point to capture researchers’ perceptions of API stability, expectations for future access, and strategies for coping with potential loss of a key data source. Indeed, the phase-out of CT served as a natural inflection point that allowed us to examine how the academic community reacted in real time to the loss of a major data access tool. Although our research protocol focused on understanding researchers’ engage- ments with CT specifically, we also gathered comparative reflections on other social media APIs. As such, this paper offers a partial cross-platform perspective, with an emphasis on researchers’ experiences navigating APIs as instruments for scholarly inquiry.

## Data analysis

Interviews were recorded using the record-to-computer fea- ture on Zoom and the audio was transcribed using an online transcription service (Descript). Identifying information was removed from transcripts and participants were as- signed an ID number (e.g. P1, P2). All transcripts were checked for accuracy three times by the first author. The transcripts were then imported into the qualitative coding software ATLAS.ti for analysis and coding. An iterative coding strategy was employed, following a constructivist grounded theory approach (Charmaz, 2006). The first au- thor conducted initial open coding of all transcripts, identi- fying in-vivo codes and preliminary patterns in participants’ descriptions of their practices with CT. Drawing on the con- ceptual framework of Seeing like an API, which we devel- oped through engagement with infrastructure studies literature (Scott, 1998; Singh and Jackson, 2021; Vertesi,

2012), subsequent rounds of focused coding identified three primary themes: (1) optimizing API capabilities, (2) extend- ing data visibility through supplementary methods, and (3) verifying platform outputs. These themes were actively constructed through our theoretical engagement with the data and our analytical focus on how researchers navigate epistemic constraints. A data matrix was used to cross- check higher-order themes for consistency across partici- pants and to identify negative cases or exceptions. All three authors met regularly to discuss coding decisions, refine theme definitions, and ensure analytical rigor. Quotes in- cluded in the manuscript were lightly edited for clarity. Findings
## Understanding and making sense of CrowdTangle

limitations

To understand how the CT API-shaped research practices, participants were asked about their perceptions and experi- ences using the tool throughout the research process. The majority of participants reported using CT because it was the only tool available to gather large amounts of data from Meta platforms, particularly Facebook and Instagram. Participants highlighted the absence of alterna- tive instruments that facilitated the kind of access to data that CT provided, emphasizing that it was the only option for access to large-scale datasets of organic content from public Facebook pages, groups, and Instagram profiles. A few participants referenced the Cambridge Analytica scan- dal, referring to the unauthorized harvesting of personal data from millions of Facebook users, with one participant noting how after this, “Meta blocked everything for us and there was no other way to get access to data except through CrowdTangle” (P2). While all of our participants recognized CT’s utility, they were also acutely aware of its limitations. All

participants noted how CT provided only partial visibility into the platform and allowed them to only see “the tip of the iceberg” (P2) or “snippets of information…not the full picture” (P4) because “the database of CrowdTangle is not a complete database of pages and groups on Facebook and Instagram” (P7). The majority of participants demon- strated a deep familiarity with the boundaries of CT’s visi- bility, and how these parameters translated to the possibilities and limitations of conducting their research. When speaking about the limitations, participants empha- sized the importance of framing research questions in align- ment with limitations and noted that CT’s effectiveness depended heavily on the user’s experience with the tool. In other words, those with a deep understanding of CT were better equipped to decide when additional data was ne- cessary and to recognize when critical data might be missing. Conversely, some participants noted how less experi- enced users might misinterpret CT outputs, mistaking abun- dance for completeness. As one participant explained, the volume of data in CT “will tend to create, sometimes, the feeling that you are getting it right” (P7). Others empha- sized that the volume of data available through CT could give a false sense of comprehensiveness, leading research- ers to overestimate what could be reliably studied. Our par- ticipants suggested how effective use of the tool involves what we conceptualize as Seeing like an API—recognizing the parameters of the partial view it provides, learning when its limitations require extending visibility into data through other means, and avoiding conclusions that extend beyond those boundaries. Participants identified several key limitations in their use of CT largely stemming from restricted access to certain types of data and metadata. The most commonly cited con- cern was the lack of access to public comments on posts. However, the motivations behind wanting access to com- ments varied. Some researchers noted that users increas- ingly share links in comments rather than in main posts, meaning the absence of comment data impedes efforts to trace link-sharing behavior. Others emphasized that com- ments are often where users express opinions, reactions, or disagreements, making them crucial for analyzing en- gagement dynamics and the public reception of content. Several participants also expressed interest in conducting semantic analyses of comment sections to study user re- sponses, identify instances of hate speech, or detect misin- formation, phenomena that are frequently found in comment threads. Without access to this layer of discourse, many felt their ability to understand and interpret how users interact with content was significantly hindered. While CT provides basic engagement metrics such as the number of likes, shares, and reactions, participants pointed out that these signals alone offer little insight into user in- tent. P1 explained that engagement metrics alone cannot disambiguate user intent: “It’s not enough to just analyze

the kind of reaction or likes because you don’t understand if people are liking or sharing the content because they agree or because maybe they find they are of the opposite parties.” This observation reveals a fundamental limitation in how CT structures visibility into user behavior. The API pro- vides quantified engagement (i.e. likes, shares, reactions) but strips away the discursive context that would make those behaviors interpretable. A “like” could signal agree- ment, ironic engagement, hate-watching, or algorithmic ob- ligation; without access to comment textual data, researchers are limited in their ability to make claims about important aspects of user sentiment. Many participants sug- gested that access to comments would be more informative than just reaction or like data, as it would aid interpretation, improve comprehensiveness, and open new lines of inquiry. Another recurring limitation was the follower-count threshold. CT only provided data on public pages, groups, and accounts that exceeded a certain number of followers. Several participants expressed frustration that publicly shared content was excluded from analysis simply because it did not meet these minimum-audience size requirements. One researcher from a country with a population size about 1/60th that of the U.S. described this as “a very U.S.-centric way of creating a database” (P12), explaining that local ac- counts, while modest in reach by global standards, may be highly influential within their own national or cultural con- text. Another participant described abandoning a study on vaping promotion due to the follower threshold:

“When you go to CrowdTangle…the data will be extremely limited…people do not have the expected number of followers, so they are not included in CrowdTangle. So how can I under- stand how vaping is promoted on social media, on Instagram, if I don’t have full access? So that’s a huge limitation. I cannot reach sound conclusions…It’s really hard. We can just get a re- flection, but not the full picture.” (P2)

Participants also encountered challenges accessing media content. Although CT provides URLs for embedded images and videos, it restricts their download and storage, thereby limiting the potential for programmatic analysis. P13 de- scribed attempting to analyze visual content:

“We wanted to look at photos… The API made that really hard to do so actually that was a pretty serious limitation, and I don’t really see why we shouldn’t be able to do that, if we could ac- cess the post and their public posts anyway…We could view it on the link in the data, but could we download it?…Their API and their platform has been set up to prevent that.” (P13)

This tension between what researchers could technically view (URLs to public images) and what they could analyt- ically use (downloaded, processable images) highlights how CT provided visibility into the existence of visual con- tent, while preventing the computational analysis that

would make that content tractable at scale. These con- straints not only impacted the methodological scope of indi- vidual projects but also shaped research agendas more broadly, particularly in contexts involving local, visual, or interaction-based analysis. Still, the majority of participants described CT as a valuable and usable tool, provided that research questions were appropriately scoped to match the affordances of the API. As one participant put it:

“As far as it is not comprehensive, it’s comprehensive enough to answer many research questions. And of course, it depends on the research question you’re working on” (P7).

## Optimizing, extending, and verifying platform data

To understand how researchers responded to the limitations of the CT API, we asked participants to describe the strat- egies they employed to navigate and address these chal- lenges. Their responses highlight three main practices: optimizing the tool’s capabilities, extending its data visibil- ity, and verifying the accuracy of its outputs. These three practices represent distinct responses to the API’s imposed optic: optimizing works within it, extending attempts to see beyond it, and verifying interrogates its reliability.

Optimizing application programming interface data. Participants described various efforts to maximize the util- ity of CT, including engaging with community support re- sources, requesting new features, and participating in workshops and forums organized by the CT team. CT orga- nized several popular activities, including onboarding ses- sions and monthly webinars. The CT team also facilitated discussions on how to access and interpret data, as well as how to request new features, through a dedicated Slack channel. The channel de facto enabled real-time problem-solving. It was created and managed by the CT team, and access was granted to researchers, journalists, and other power users via personal invitation. These activ- ities, including the Slack channel, not only facilitated tech- nical support but also created an informal knowledge-sharing network that shaped research design and use of the tool. One participant described the Slack community as a “common brain trust” (P15):

“If one researcher wanted to know how to best return results for a particular topic and thought that their keyword search or Boolean query was not structured correctly they could ask… there were things… I would find periodically where I was like…why is what’s being returned in the historical data not the same as what I’m seeing on my screen? I could very quickly reach a person there and, you know, sort out…was there a rounding dynamic happening somewhere? Which of these two sources is more authoritative if I’m putting it into a paper?” (P15)

These exchanges served dual functions: they provided im- mediate technical support (query construction, trouble- shooting discrepancies) while also building collective interpretive competence about CT’s quirks and limitations. The question “which of these two sources is more authorita- tive?” reveals how even experienced users encountered am- biguities requiring communal resolution. Such knowledge —when to trust historical data versus live displays, how rounding affects counts—couldn’t be gleaned from docu- mentation alone. It emerged through shared problem-solving, creating a sense of distributed expertise. P15 continued:

“But the other thing that was nice about the meetings… was that you could see what other people were doing with the tool…I am not an extremism researcher, nobody in my team is really an extremism researcher, but there were people in the group who were… they would talk through some of their methodologies and what they were doing on CrowdTangle, and it would give us ideas for things that we could ask about, or…methods that we could use to incorporate into our stuff.” (P15)

Beyond technical troubleshooting, the community facili- tated methodological cross-pollination across disciplinary boundaries. The majority of participants noted that attend- ing these webinars was essential for learning how to opti- mize CT data in their research. Several participants described the community as open and collaborative and de- scribed feeling as though the CT leadership team had con- structed a space for them to request new features and point out weaknesses and areas of improvement for the tool. Several participants who were highly involved in the CT community expressed that they felt like they were granted an active role in co-shaping some of the evolution of CT. As one participant noted:

“Some of the evolution was actually responsive to some of the needs from academics and journalists. So CrowdTangle now supports, like, you can search for images, you can do a bunch of other searches around location and time and these kinds of things. And a lot of that was sort of borne out from conversa- tions that researchers and journalists…had with…the develo- pers behind CrowdTangle’s platform.” (P8)

However, participants also pointed out that certain function- alities which they had long requested, like the ability to ac- cess user comments on posts, were not provided. In this way, the agency they had was real but limited. Extending platform data visibility beyond the application programming interface. When CT’s native functionality proved insufficient, many researchers developed strategies to extend data visibility beyond what the API could return. These included web scraping, manual collection, and

integrating data from other APIs. CT was often used as a starting point—a scaffold to identify relevant cases or en- tities that researchers would then explore more deeply using unsanctioned or supplementary methods. Participants often had to balance their preference for using the official CT API with the need for comprehensive data collection. As P14 explained:

“Certainly, I would like to use the official product. In our case, the official product is insufficient because it doesn’t deliver images or videos. So, we have to build solutions in order to ex- tract those. But we use the official products as the initial data in order to structure our method around, we need to know what post to search in order to scrape them.” (P14)

These workarounds were often considered necessary to meet academic standards for comprehensiveness and repro- ducibility. Even researchers with formal data-sharing agree- ments found they could not obtain complete datasets without resorting to scraping tools, which often risked ac- count bans, as P18 explained:

“If you wanted to be like, ‘okay, I just want to have an archive of what that account posted,’ that’s not a thing you can do on CrowdTangle. I can’t get every post. I can’t get the text of it, every comment, the engagement data, which, you know, I can get with other APIs. So instead, you have to do things like, even though we have a corporate agreement with Facebook to use this tool for research purposes, we end up scraping things from the public facing tool, because it’s the only way that we can get enough data to really run an analysis and keep an archive for posterity of these accounts.” (P18)

Despite being a widely used strategy, web scraping was de- scribed by the majority of participants as precarious. While some researchers routinely used scraping to supplement their data, others avoided it altogether, noting that they con- sidered it inherently unreliable. One participant noted how with web scraping “there’s just a lot more potential sources of things that go wrong…it’s a little bit more archaic and I think researchers generally use it as a last resort” (P9). Some participants also expressed worry, and sometimes fear, that scraping would lead to account bans and prevent them from conducting research altogether. For instance, P18 noted how:

“We’re talking like bumping your head against rate limits, worrying about your account getting banned for crawling… In the case of Facebook groups and stuff like that, crawling is just—Ugh, it’s, it’s so painful…just being able to archive stuff so that we can do deeper analysis is very hard… that’s not something that the CrowdTangle API has ever really helped with much… That’s been the major pain point for us…being able to do archival and putting things into a format where we

can really do proper time series and our own kind of big data type analysis… that’s been the difficult thing for us.” (P18)

Others emphasized the instability of scraping as a method, describing it as a “cat and mouse” game (P7) that made data collection fragile and error-prone. These reflections underscore the risks and challenges involved in extending visibility of data beyond that which is provided by platform- sanctioned tools. At the same time, they highlight how CT’s design implicitly steers researchers toward particular meth- odological choices, favoring certain types of data collection while complicating or disincentivizing others.

Verifying platform data. Beyond filling gaps, participants also used scraping and manual checks to verify the accuracy of CT data. Trust in the platform’s outputs was not taken for granted. Participants expressed uncertainty about whether CT outputs were complete and accurate. P10 articulated this as a trust problem:

“You’re relying on, unless you’ve coded it yourself, you’re relying on somebody… they can code it for something, a com- pletely different kind of case and it worked perfectly for them…then you put it into your research project, and it really misses a lot, right?” (P10)

P10’s observation highlights an asymmetry fundamental to API-based research: researchers depend on infrastructure whose internal operations remain opaque. Unlike self- authored code that can be inspected and debugged, APIs function as black boxes. When they fail (e.g. returning in- complete results, missing data, producing inconsistencies), researchers often cannot determine whether the problem lies in their query construction, the API’s functioning, or the underlying data. This opacity makes even experienced researchers question their results. P10 continued:

“So, there’s a huge amount of trust…with what we give to Facebook with CrowdTangle as well. I think it’sreal,real,prob- lematic… you kind of are placing all this trust in whoever’scre- ating the API and the fact that it does it diligently.” (P10)

Participants addressed this issue by triangulating CT outputs with other data sources. This included using scraping tools to re-query specific pages or posts, manually checking public- facing content, and comparing results across different API calls or time periods. Participants noted how these forms of verification sometimes exposed discrepancies—such as missing posts, engagement counts that did not match live data, or inconsistencies across API endpoints. For example, one participant recalled discovering a data omission:

“We identified a bug in CrowdTangle where there was some data missing and we wound up scraping Facebook to validate our CrowdTangle data and found that there was in fact a

bug…Scraping Facebook was pretty much the way we vali- dated data when we doubted it.” (P11)

However, another participant pointed out that this was a key strength of the CT API: all the data it provided could be ex- ternally validated, even if it took a lot of effort. They con- trasted this with other Meta-owned APIs, they explained:

“With other Meta services, like the ad library API, you cannot do that. And I can tell you from experience that that data is in- complete depending on the version of the API you query, how you query it. We’ve had enormous gaps in our data using the ad library API, which is actually why…we went to organic data in order to make sure that we could verify that it was complete be- cause we wanted to do a study on ads, but we had missing data all over the place.” (P14)

Other participants reported using manual data collection as a way to gain additional access to data, with some high- lighting how this enabled them to engage deeper with the data, as one participant notes:

“There’s nothing like looking at the data yourself…there’s nothing like actually going through the process of…putting eyes on what you’re looking at because…You look at it and you might see, ‘okay, these are the subsets or the data points that I’m looking for’ and you see, ‘okay, they figure there and there’ and then you see in between, ‘oh look, there are other ones that are similar but different,’ that wouldn’t be cov- ered in the API search that I’d done, but I want to include them in my study because they’re relevant. And so, you know what I mean? There’s a little bit about actually immersing yourself in the case that I think that APIs can undermine.” (P10)

These practices of verification served a dual function: they exposed the limitations and occasional failures of API infra- structure, while also allowing researchers to better calibrate their interpretations. In this way, verification was not simply a matter of quality control, but a methodological stance that foregrounded the provisional and situated nature of API-generated data in scholarly research.

## Application programming interfaces as infrastructure

Our second research question asked: How do API infra- structures shape research design, collaboration, and epi- stemic practices in social media scholarship? To answer this, we examined how participants understood the broader effects of API access and limitations—not just on individual projects, but on the structural conditions of social media re- search as a field. Their reflections revealed three key dy- namics: first, that API access is unevenly distributed, reinforcing institutional and geographic hierarchies; se- cond, that informal infrastructures (such as Slack groups or peer-to-peer workarounds) emerged to support data

access and interpretation; and third, that API constraints re- shaped collaborative practices, peer review norms, and broader forms of epistemic labor.

API inequities: differential access & academic hierarchies in social media research. Differential access to CT emerged as a prominent concern for our participants. Two forms of differential access surfaced: differential access among re- searchers (i.e. who received access to CT) and differential access within the API itself (i.e. what data CT explicitly in- cluded or excluded). One participant also observed dispar- ities in informal access and stated how closer affiliation to the leadership team led to unequal access to informal re- sources, and noted “there’s no way I’ve got the same level of both integration with the CrowdTangle crew and also… that kind of research community where I can discuss this data in a meaningful way and integrate it more” (P10). While most participants expressed surprise at how straightforward it was to acquire access to CT, those af- filiated with well-known institutions acknowledged that this experience was not universal. Several emphasized that the absence of formal pathways for official data access via APIs had serious implications for early-career scholars and those at smaller or less-resourced institutions. As P14 explained:

“I can understand the platform side that they want to have… well established, credible researchers who can get the job done, but it absolutely feeds a sort of hierarchy within aca- demia where institutions like Stanford, physical proximity to these platforms, and large institutions, big names, get published access. They get to publish in Science, in Nature…The rest of us are dealing with these kinds of hacky solutions to try to make a career.” (P14)

Several participants noted a direct link between the lack of official access to APIs and the need for “hacky” (P14) or “creative” (P5) solutions such as web scraping that were of- ten seen as “a last resort” (P9). In addition to individual car- eer implications, participants noted how unequal access shapes research at a structural level. As P11 reflected:

“I think it’s a real shame because I think CrowdTangle has been very helpful for research… It never really lived up to its poten- tial though, because so few people had access to it. It was really restricted in terms of…who got it…I think that was really prob- lematic that so few people had access to it and the people who had access to it…was not randomly distributed. It was the or- ganizations that had been around the longest and…had the most power and authority, which I understand. But that has the effect of limiting it, when you freeze out institutions that are younger…more likely to serve people of color, it…shifts the kinds of questions that get asked.” (P11)

One participant from the Global South observed that their affiliation with a European university was the only reason they had access to CT. This dual vantage point, they noted, made the underlying inequities clearer:

“They are treated different, scholars from…well recognized univer- sities and the ones from the global south, that has also issues…I think it’s important, that’s the thing, that some scholars… from the global south are using scraping instead of the official API, right?…I think it’s because these limitations, then people need to be creative and try to download the data somehow.” (P5)

P5 observed that many web scraping scripts for supplemen- tary data are developed by researchers who do not have of- ficial API access, often from specific countries and smaller institutions. Yet these same tools were fragile. Meta’s plat- form changes often broke scraping scripts, rendering them unreliable or unusable. And for those relying on these meth- ods, maintaining functionality required constant vigilance and labor. As P5 recalled:

“I remember once scraping code that I had from Facebook groups, and I could have the comments and everything, et ce- tera, that I didn’t have in CrowdTangle for example. However, it worked for, you know, two, three months and then suddenly Facebook changes the code and then you need to adapt it or start from scratch. So, this is a big challenge. On the other side, when we send a paper that we did using scraping data, then there is a lot of reviewers that rejected our paper because they say that this is against the terms of use of platforms, so you cannot do that.” (P5)

While most participants understood that scraping was com- monly used as an alternative to APIs to gain greater visibil- ity on platforms, they also noted that for some researchers, it was the only way to gain access to data. Most participants noted how platforms’ attempts to thwart scrapers (e.g. ac- count bans and infrastructural changes) and academic pub- lication policies that restrict scraping were generally inconvenient; however, a few emphasized that these obsta- cles had more severe consequences for those without privi- leged access to the API. Indeed, despite being quick to list the limitations of CT, several researchers expressed a sense of appreciation and awareness that CT provided them far more data than other platforms and far more than what other researchers had to work with. Still, there was a prevailing sense among the majority of participants that uneven access among researchers should not be the case; nearly all partici- pants agreed that access should not depend on affiliation, geography, or institutional prestige and voiced support for industry standards that could ensure more equitable and consistent access. A few suggested that broader access to tools like CT would not only advance public-interest re- search but also help mitigate the inequities and hierarchies currently embedded in the research ecosystem.

CrowdTangle and academic practices. Peer Review. To examine how CT-shaped scholarly publishing, we asked participants about their experiences using CT data during peer review, both as authors and reviewers. Several partici- pants recalled instances where, in their role as reviewers, they re-ran queries in CT to check consistency and validate reported findings. Many saw this as a valuable feature of CT that enhanced research replicability. As one participant explained:

“One time I remember, I just verified something that was writ- ten on one paper in terms of the number of posts reported by a certain query. And, when I replicated the query, the number was completely different, which, on one hand is fine, but the difference was too large, which made me think that the way they wrote the query was not exactly the way they wanted it to be written.” (P7)

P7’s comment reflects a common understanding among par- ticipants: small discrepancies in the returned results from the same query done at different times were accepted and even expected, due to post-deletion or moderation. However, large inconsistencies sometimes raised more sub- stantial concerns, such as about research question formula- tion and design, methodological approaches, the reliability of query construction, and interpretation of API outputs. Most participants did not report major challenges during peer review when submitting papers that used CT data. In many cases, reviewers simply asked basic clarifying questions (e.g. “What is CrowdTangle?”). However, one participant voiced concerns about the depth of critical engagement re- viewers bring to API-based research. As one participant noted:

“Reviewers they do pull it up, they are concerned about it. I think there is a real problem, because…reviewers…they are not necessarily thinking about…what can’t quant stuff tell us? You know, they are not thinking about those broader ques- tions about: what are the limitations of this research method in general?” (P10)

Participants also pointed out that reviewers who lacked ac- cess to CT, or were unfamiliar with its functionalities, were less likely to critically assess the appropriateness of queries or the limits of the data. Unlike active CT users, these re- viewers could not replicate or verify findings, which limited the kinds of feedback provided by peer review. This high- lights how unequal access and uneven familiarity with re- search infrastructure have implications for many stages of the research process, shaping not only who is able to engage in certain kinds of research (and thus the kinds of research questions that are asked), but also how that research is eval- uated and where it is published. Collaboration Practices. While we’ve discussed how CT fostered a community that some viewed as a “common

brain trust” (P15) for generating queries and methods, we also found that APIs reshape collaboration and transparency norms within the broader research community. For in- stance, a few participants described how the fear of platform retaliation shaped their willingness to engage openly with the research community. P18 explained:

“For scraping in general, mostly we do our own tooling… I would say, we open source three quarters of that and get some collaboration with other institutions. There is a risk to that, which is that if those sites don’t want you to be… scraping data or pulling things out of their public API, [open source is] kind of a roadmap for what to do to prevent that from happen- ing…that’s just like an unfortunate side effect of collabor- ation… the more open you are with it, the more that, you know, the organization that you’re trying to pull data from will try and use that to stop it from happening.” (P18)

This example illustrates how platform-mediated data access can shape epistemic practices by constraining how methods and tools are not only designed but shared. As P18 explained, researchers must balance the benefits of openness, such as fos- tering collaboration and advancing knowledge, and the poten- tial risks of making methods visible to platforms that may then respond by restricting or blocking their access. Rumors of CrowdTangle Shutdown. When asked about Meta’s rumored shutdown of CT, most participants ex- pressed deep concern about the implications for their own re- search and for the broader field of social media scholarship. Many described CT as essential to public-interest research, and characterized its potential closure as destabilizing, frus- trating, and disheartening. Beyond personal disruption, parti- cipants framed the shutdown as symptomatic of broader infrastructural precarity in platform research. P14 explained:

“It’s had a huge impact on me recently in terms of, what is my research design that I can promise for this grant?…It’s difficult. It really puts us in a bad spot because we don’t know what we can, A. Research and B. Promise to funders, which in a sense, I think means that Facebook’s strategy is working, if there is a strategy, to stop research on certain topics, right? Because you can’t promise it…you don’t want to risk developing a re- search design around it that can’t be done. What do you do if you win this money and you hire two PhDs and all of a sudden, the data’s not available, that’s messing with people’s lives, right?…I think an ethical requirement on researchers to fulfill what they promise, not so much from a research perspective, but in terms of the human resources and people moving coun- tries or cities in the U.S.” (P14)

Several participants also emphasized how CT played an im- portant role in academic training and pedagogy. Some used CT data in the classroom, downloading limited data to share with students for use in assignments and projects. For these

participants, the potential loss of CT meant not only losing a research tool, but also a teaching resource. As P12 noted:

“The major pro of the tool is that I can get to the kind of data that I need to keep doing my research and to keep doing my teaching, so it’s rather worrying that they’re supposedly shut- ting this down.” (P12)

Others highlighted how CT enabled early-career scholars to gain hands-on experience with real-world platform data. For instance, P9 described how sharing CT data with an undergraduate research assistant led to a project that helped the student gain admission to graduate school. Similarly, P8 reflected on the tool’s accessibility for novice researchers:

“If I wanted to get my PhD students spun up to do something in CrowdTangle, I can add them to my CrowdTangle user dash- board, and they can log into Facebook, access the CrowdTangle website, and they don’t need really specialized knowledge to start doing search using the web interface… that’s, I think, pretty significant in terms of like researcher ex- ploration and accelerating research in the space.” (P8)

Several participants warned that the closure of CT could produce long-term shifts in the kinds of research that are feasible. In particular, they anticipated a disproportionate shift toward platforms with more accessible APIs, such as Twitter/X, and increased reliance on ad hoc data collection methods like scraping. One participant warned of the broader impact this might have on scientific research:

“With the sort of unofficial scraping… each research group is going to do it by themselves, essentially, so there might be a lot of overlap and duplication between efforts. The scrapers themselves, of course, will all have to be developed and may not be entirely reli- able, so data quality might decline…It gets a bit more Wild West… It gets less standardized and less controlled. It makes it…alotmore difficult to verify any of the research coming out because only that group, with that particular scraper tool will have been able to gather the data. In theory, with a piece of research that’susing CrowdTangle, if I see that, I can go in and gather the same data and verify and compare my results with another groups and kind of see if they’ve done the right thing in analyzing their data. None of that is really possible if everyone kind of uses their own bespoke tools for data gathering…It becomes a lot more messy and less standardized, and just less repeatable and testable.” (P4)

Discussion

## CrowdTangle’s application programming interface as

## an epistemic infrastructure: lessons learned and

future directions

Since the completion of this research, CT was sunset in August 2024, joining a broader pattern of platform API

closures including Twitter/X’s termination of free API ac- cess in 2023 and Reddit’s increased API restrictions in

2023. This shift from relatively open API access to a “post-API” environment—characterized by regulatory mandates (such as the DSA), alongside increased reliance on alternative methods such as web scraping and data dona- tions—makes our documentation of CT both a snapshot of a vanishing infrastructure and a framework for understanding emerging access modalities. We investigated how researchers perceive, navigate, and address the limitations of the CT API—specifically regard- ing data availability, stability, and accuracy (RQ1)—and how APIs, as key infrastructural components, shape schol- arly practices and research agendas in the field of social media studies (RQ2). To interpret these dynamics, we intro- duced the framework of Seeing like an API, which high- lights how researchers come to understand and adapt to the epistemic boundaries imposed by platform data infra- structures. This framework draws attention to how access architectures shape not only what data can be retrieved, but what types of research become legible, what methods are possible, and whose expertise is recognized. One high- level goal of this work was to understand which aspects of platform-mediated data access infrastructures effectively support social media research, and which introduce friction, uncertainty, and inequity. Our findings can inform emer- ging data governance models mediated by independent third parties, either acting alone or in collaboration with platforms (the DSA in the EU, ICPSR’s facilitated Meta Content Library, etc.), which seek to facilitate fairer, more transparent, and more equitable access to platform data. Our findings confirm prior research highlighting the crit- ical role APIs play as epistemic infrastructures shaping the boundaries and possibilities of scholarly knowledge pro- duction (Acker and Donovan, 2019; Bowker and Star, 1999; Hemphill et al., 2021). Previous studies emphasize how platform-controlled APIs inherently limit researchers’ autonomy by structuring what data is accessible, who has access, and ultimately, what research questions can be pur- sued (Bruns, 2019; Puschmann, 2019). Our research sup- ports this assertion, as participants repeatedly noted how CT’s follower-count thresholds and lack of comment data constrained their ability to pursue certain topics or compre- hensively study user interactions. For example, a researcher exploring vaping promotion abandoned their project be- cause accounts of interest did not meet CT’s threshold for inclusion in the dataset (P2). Similarly, scholars interested in audience engagement described significant interpretive challenges due to the omission of public comment data, em- phasizing that basic engagement metrics alone could not re- liably reveal user intent or sentiment (P1). However, our findings move beyond diagnosing limita- tions to document how researchers developed situated strat- egies of adaptation, optimization, and verification in response. Participants employed both informal and formal

knowledge-sharing networks, such as community Slack channels and regular webinars organized by CT, which functioned as a “common brain trust” (P15). Such networks enabled researchers to collaboratively troubleshoot queries, share interpretive strategies, and advocate for enhancements to the API. Yet, these networks were not universally access- ible. Scholars without close institutional or personal con- nections to the CT community often lacked awareness or access, exacerbating existing academic hierarchies and dif- ferential opportunities (P5, P10, P14). This highlights an implicit inequity embedded in current infrastructures: ac- cess to essential interpretive and methodological resources remains unevenly distributed, further perpetuating existing disparities within the academic community (Acker and Donovan, 2019; Puschmann, 2019). In parallel, researchers frequently sought to extend CT’s visibility through supplementary methods such as scraping, manual archiving, or integrating other APIs, and to verify its outputs by cross-checking against independent datasets. These practices and situated adaptations demonstrate that Seeing like an API is not a passive optic imposed from above but an ongoing negotiation: researchers appropriate the platform’s vantage, repurpose it, and erect auxiliary sight-lines around it. Our findings illustrate that agency does not disappear in platform-governed environments; it is redirected into continual acts of repair, supplementation, and strategic caution. Yet, such agency is double-edged. Each workaround requires additional labor, introduces idio- syncratic coverage errors, and narrows replicability, as one participant noted, with such tactics “it becomes a lot more difficult to verify any of the research coming out because only that group, with that particular scraper tool, will have been able to gather the data” (P4). In effect, while re- searchers’ methodological ingenuity sustains inquiry under imperfect conditions, it can also normalize the very infra- structural constraints that made such ingenuity necessary in the first place. By embedding compensatory, adaptive practices (i.e. scrapers, manual audits, private heuristics) into standard methods, researcher agency both resists and reinscribes the platform’s epistemic terms: it opens mo- mentary windows beyond the API’s view while simultan- eously reaffirming the centrality of that view as the object around which methodological creativity must orbit. These dynamics highlight the importance of designing access sys- tems that explicitly account for the tacit labor and informal knowledge networks on which researchers rely, so that in- equities and friction in platform-mediated data access are not simply reproduced in new governance models. Another significant finding relates to platform transpar- ency, specifically regarding data deletions and omissions. Researchers repeatedly encountered unexplained discrepan- cies between API outputs and manually collected or scraped datasets, with no clear indication of whether missing posts or engagements were the result of user deletion, moder- ation, or other filtering (P11, P14). The lack of transparency

about data omissions, including why certain posts or en- gagements were excluded, created persistent ambiguity about the quality and representativeness of datasets. This opacity not only complicated methodological decisions but also undermined researchers’ confidence in interpreting their results (Sen et al., 2021; Wu and Taneja, 2021). Frameworks such as Sen et al.’s (2021) Total Error Framework for Digital Traces of Human Behavior (TED-On) offer a useful way to classify error sources spe- cific to social media, including coverage issues, processing faults, and construct validity concerns. These categories are valuable for describing how data infrastructures like CT may differ from, or incompletely capture, the phenomena they are used to study. In some cases, such discrepancies may indeed reflect platform-level filtering or limitations in- herent in the API’s design; in others, they may arise from researchers’ incomplete familiarity with the tool’s features, the boundaries of its data scope, or the types of inferences that can reasonably be drawn from its outputs. However, TED-On does not directly address the politics of omission that was often a concern for the majority of our participants. Indeed, CT limitations were often not interpreted as meas- urement or representation errors, the kind of technical short- coming that could be corrected statistically or noted in a methods section, but instead were framed as products of in- ternal platform decisions: curated invisibilities shaped by legal, reputational, or commercial considerations. Framing these exclusions as technical shortcomings alone risks, as Acker and Donovan (2019) and van der Vlist et al. (2022) argue, naturalizing platform asymmetries. It treats the boundaries of data visibility as neutral or inevitable, rather than contingent and contestable. What is frequently referred to as “noise” is often the consequence of deliberate filtering logic, rooted in proprietary systems, opaque policies, or commercial priorities. Such findings underscore the import- ance of transparent decision-making processes in data gov- ernance, as transparency directly influences the validity and reliability of scholarly research. Recognizing these infrastructural dynamics does not im- ply adversarial critique of platforms; rather, it provides con- structive insights into how data governance practices could evolve. Platforms are private entities balancing diverse de- mands, including user privacy, commercial interests, and regulatory compliance (John and Nissenbaum, 2019). The emergence of regulatory frameworks such as the European Union’s DSA, which mandates that VLOPs pro- vide vetted researchers access to publicly accessible data (European Commission, 2022), represents a significant shift from voluntary to legally required access. However, early DSA implementation raises questions about whether man- dated access through platform-controlled interfaces like Meta’s Content Library and API reproduces the epistemo- logical constraints we document or enables genuinely inde- pendent inquiry. Simultaneously, researchers have turned to alternative “post-API” methods including web scraping,

data donations, and browser-based capture tools (Trezza,

2023). Each method introduces distinct epistemological af- fordances and access barriers: scraping bypasses platform control but violates Terms of Service and requires specia- lized technical expertise (Sandvig et al., 2014); data dona- tions center user agency but shift the unit of analysis and introduce sampling challenges. The Seeing like an API framework we develop has broader applicability to these ac- cess modalities, in that each shape what can be known about platforms, who can produce that knowledge, and whose ex- pertise is legitimized. This underscores the importance of methodological pluralism (Rogers, 2013) as different meth- ods make visible different facets of platform systems, each with partial, positioned perspectives shaped by their own constraints. Rather than privileging API access, we argue for multiple access points that enable triangulation and sup- port diverse research questions and communities. Nevertheless, third-party mediation of data governance presents a promising opportunity to reconcile these compet- ing priorities while enhancing transparency, equity, and scholarly utility. Drawing on our findings, we offer the fol- lowing specific recommendations for data governance bod- ies facilitating platform research access across multiple access modalities, applicable whether through APIs, data donation platforms, third-party intermediaries, or other ac- cess mechanisms:
- Universal Knowledge-Sharing Networks: Establish and actively maintain open, inclusive, and widely ad- vertised researcher communities or forums that facili- tate shared interpretive strategies, methodological collaboration, and troubleshooting support.
- Transparent Data Visibility Decisions: Clearly docu- ment and publicly communicate the rationale behind decisions determining which data are included or ex- cluded, enabling researchers to accurately assess data- set comprehensiveness and reliability.
- Support for Verification and Validation: Enable researchers to systematically verify data quality and API outputs through transparent and auditable verifi- cation mechanisms.
- Equitable and Consistent Access: Implement stan- dardized and transparent criteria for researcher ac- cess, independent of institutional affiliation or geographic location, to reduce disparities and democ- ratize opportunities for scholarly inquiry. By implementing these practices, third-party governance
entities can foster more robust, inclusive, and transparent data access environments, strengthening the foundations of rigorous and equitable scholarly research on social media platforms. Having outlined recommendations for future data govern- ance, we now turn to the limitations of our study. This study concentrated on a single API, potentially limiting the general applicability of findings to other social media APIs due to the

variance in platform structural affordances and API access policies. Focusing on one API allowed us to investigate how certain constraints in API designs led to specificprac- tices; however, future research could provide a more detailed and comprehensive cross-platform analysis of social media APIs. This research also explores the perceptions of research- ers with privileged access to CT, and while we probed parti- cipants about their perceptions on CT’s conditions of access, to comprehensively understand how differential access perpe- tuates inequalities, understanding how researchers without privileged access conduct social media research is an import- ant focus for future research.

Conclusion

Our study offers an empirical account of how researchers adapted to the CT API’s constraints through reconciliatory and resistant workarounds, shifts in inquiry, and epistemic trade-offs. We show that the CT API not only limited what data could be retrieved but also influenced how researchers in- terpreted those data, collaborated with peers, and defined vi- able research questions. By documenting these adaptations, this study provides both a snapshot of a vanishing API infra- structure and an application of the Seeing like an API frame- work to trace how the technical architecture, governance rules, and interpretive communities surrounding a tool collect- ively shape the production of scholarly knowledge. Looking forward, the design of data access infrastructures—whether through DSA-mandated access, platform APIs, or third-party data trusts—must grapple with the tensions our participants navigated: between data comprehensiveness and privacy pro- tection, between open access and platform retaliation, and be- tween standardization and flexibility. The Seeing like an API framework reminds us that data infrastructures are never neu- tral conduits but active participants in shaping what questions can be asked, whose expertise is valued, and what knowledge claims are deemed legitimate. As the field moves beyond the CT era, the challenge is not simply to restore access but to build more equitable, transparent, and epistemically robust in- frastructures that support diverse research communities and enable critical, independent scholarship on platforms.
